# Facilitator Reference — Example AI Counterarguments

Worked examples for comparison ONLY. Do not hand these to students before they attempt their
own counterarguments — they're here so you can hold student work (and the AI output students
generate) against a strong model. Each uses Prompt A from `prompts/phase-2-counter.md`.

## Example Argument 2 (Late Work)

### Prompt used
> You are helping me, a high school student, learn to build a strong counterargument. Here is
> the original argument: [Riley's argument from example-argument-2-late-work.md]. Here are the
> weaknesses I found in it: the brother's class is just one example; "real life has no deadlines"
> seems wrong; it assumes everyone should get the same flexibility. Write a counterargument…
> [+ the standing Prompt A instructions].

### Example response (a strong counter)
> Counterargument. First, your strongest point — that a grade should measure learning, not
> timing. I agree a grade should reflect understanding. But hitting a deadline is part of what
> the class is building, not an unrelated add-on; managing your time is a skill the course
> teaches alongside the subject, so "when you submit" isn't as disconnected from "what you
> learned" as it sounds. (Targets the claim that timing is irrelevant.)
>
> Second, the idea that deadlines punish students with harder lives is partly right — but it
> points to the wrong fix. The answer to genuine hardship is accommodations for those students
> (extensions when something real comes up), not removing deadlines for everyone, which helps
> the procrastinator exactly as much as the kid caring for a sibling. (Targets the
> overgeneralization.)
>
> Third, your brother's class doesn't show the policy caused the grades — one class is a tiny
> sample, and the results could come from an easy curriculum, a strong teacher, or a motivated
> group. (Targets the anecdote.)
>
> Finally, "real life doesn't work that way" actually cuts the other way: job applications,
> taxes, and work projects all run on hard deadlines with real consequences, so reasonable
> deadlines now are practice for that. (I left the "teachers like control" line alone — it's
> about motives, not the merits.)
>
> Most solid part of my counter: the accommodations point, because it takes the fairness concern
> seriously. Weakest part: my claim that meeting deadlines is "part of what the class teaches"
> is debatable — you could argue a grade should isolate subject mastery only.
>
> Question for you: if you wanted to honor the fairness concern but keep deadlines meaningful,
> what policy would you actually design?

## Example Argument 3 (AI Essays)

### Prompt used
> Same as Prompt A, with Sam's argument from example-argument-3-ai-essays.md pasted in and the
> weakness list left as "you find them."

### Example response (a strong counter)
> Counterargument. Start with your strongest point — employers use AI, so banning it leaves us
> unprepared. That's true and worth taking seriously, but it supports a different conclusion than
> yours. Learning to use AI well and having AI write your essay aren't the same thing. The
> assignment exists to build your thinking and writing; if the AI produces it, that skill never
> develops. Being ready for an AI workplace means being someone who can think and use AI to go
> faster — not someone who can't think once the tool is gone. (Targets the "unprepared for jobs"
> point — concedes, then redirects.)
>
> On the calculator comparison: a calculator does a step you already understand — you still decide
> what to compute. Letting AI write the whole essay automates the exact skill the assignment is
> meant to grow. I'll grant the kernel of truth — tools do get normalized, so "AI in school" will
> look different in a few years — but "it'll be normal eventually" isn't "it builds the skill now."
> (Targets the false analogy.)
>
> On "teachers can't detect it, so the rule is pointless": whether a rule is easy to enforce is
> separate from whether it's worth having. The point of doing your own writing isn't to avoid
> getting caught — it's that you're the one who loses the skill if you skip it, detectable or not.
> (Targets the enforcement move.)
>
> I left out "anyone against this doesn't understand technology," since it's aimed at the person,
> not the idea — and plenty of people who understand AI well still think students should write
> their own essays.
>
> Most solid part: the distinction between using AI and outsourcing the thinking. Weakest part:
> I'm assuming the essay's main purpose is to build the student's own writing — for assignments
> where the goal is the finished product, the AI argument gets stronger.
>
> Question for you: where's the line between "using AI as a tool" and "letting it do the work,"
> and who should decide where that line sits?
