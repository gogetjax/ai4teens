# Example Argument

Jordan is a fictional student. The analysts in this workshop are the class's own
students — Jordan is just the person whose argument they take apart.

## The argument (as posted in a class group chat)

> Honestly, our school needs to drop the no-phones-at-lunch rule. Everyone already
> uses their phone at lunch anyway — half the cafeteria is on Snapchat right now — so
> the rule does nothing except turn normal kids into rule-breakers. Lunch is our own
> time, not class time, so there's no reason a phone should be banned then. And it's
> not like phones hurt our grades: my cousin goes to a school across the state that
> allows phones all day, and they have some of the highest test scores around. The
> only reason teachers cling to this rule is that they don't actually trust us — and
> that's exactly why nobody respects the rule.

---

## Facilitator answer key (don't hand this to students)

The argument contains one genuinely reasonable point plus several planted weaknesses:

- "Everyone already does it" — appeal to common practice; a rule being broken doesn't
  show it's wrong ("is" doesn't prove "ought").
- "turns normal kids into rule-breakers" — loaded framing, not evidence.
- "Lunch is our own time" — the strongest point; genuinely reasonable, though it assumes
  the school has no legitimate interest in lunchtime phone use.
- Cousin's school — anecdotal (a sample of one), and correlation isn't causation; high
  test scores could come from many other factors.
- "Teachers don't trust us" — attacks motives instead of the rule's merits (ad hominem),
  and the ending is nearly circular.
