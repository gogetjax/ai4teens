# Student Passage B — Why Bond Prices Fall When Interest Rates Rise

> **Facilitator:** Hand the student only the passage block below. Keep the answer key.

## The passage (give to student)
> A bond's market price moves in the opposite direction to interest rates, because
> the bond's payments are locked in when it is issued, while the return investors
> can get elsewhere keeps changing. When interest rates rise above the bond's fixed
> payment rate, that fixed stream of payments becomes less attractive than what's
> newly available, so the bond can only be sold on the open market at a discount
> below its original face value — a discount sized precisely so that a new buyer's
> effective return matches the higher going rate. How much the price has to drop for
> a given rise in rates depends on the bond's "duration," roughly the average time
> until its payments arrive: bonds that pay out further in the future swing in price
> much more than bonds that pay out soon.

## Facilitator answer key (don't hand to students)

**Plain-language translation:** A bond is basically an IOU that pays a fixed amount.
Once it's issued, those payments never change. But interest rates out in the world
*do* change. If new bonds start paying more than your old one, nobody will pay full
price for your old, lower-paying bond — so to sell it, you have to drop the price
until the deal is just as good as the new bonds. That's why bond prices fall when
rates rise. And "duration" tells you how violently the price swings: bonds whose
payments come further in the future move a lot more for the same change in rates.

**Terms students will trip on:** bond (a loan you buy that pays you back over time —
an IOU); market price (what someone will actually pay for it today); interest rates
(the going return on money elsewhere); issued (when the bond is first created);
fixed payment / coupon rate (the locked-in amount it pays); face value (the bond's
stated original value); discount (sold for less than that); effective return / yield
(what you actually earn given the price you paid); duration (roughly the average wait
until you get paid); swing/sensitivity (how much the price moves).

**Prerequisites to build first:** what a bond is (an IOU that pays fixed amounts);
that you can re-sell a bond to someone else before it ends; that money has a "going
rate" elsewhere that competes with the bond; that buyers compare deals.

**Best analogy + its seam:** A "$5 off forever" coupon. It looked great when issued.
Now everyone hands out "$7 off" coupons, so your $5 one is worth less — you'd only
get someone to take it if you also knock a bit off the price to even out the deal.
*Seam:* the coupon captures "old fixed deal vs. better new deals," but doesn't
naturally show *duration* — that a longer-lasting coupon loses more value. For
duration: a longer seesaw tips further from the same push. *Seam:* you're switching
analogies, so flag that out loud.

**Common traps:** Getting the direction backwards (rates UP → prices DOWN). Thinking
the bond's payments change (they don't — that's the whole point). Confusing face
value with market price. Skipping *why* the discount is sized the way it is (to make
the new buyer's return match the market). Hand-waving duration as just "time" instead
of "average time until you're paid."

**A strong present-back sounds like:** "A bond pays fixed amounts that never change.
If interest rates out in the world go up, new bonds pay more, so my old bond is worth
less — I have to sell it at a discount so the buyer earns the same as the new rate.
Rates up, price down. And the longer until the bond pays out, the bigger the price
swing — that's duration."
