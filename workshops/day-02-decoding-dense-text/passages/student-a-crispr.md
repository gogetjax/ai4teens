# Student Passage A — CRISPR-Cas9

> **Facilitator:** Hand the student only the passage block below. Keep the answer key.

## The passage (give to student)
> CRISPR-Cas9 is an RNA-guided "molecular scissors" system adapted from a defense
> mechanism bacteria use against viruses. A short, lab-designed guide RNA is built
> to match a specific target sequence of DNA, and it steers the Cas9 protein to
> that exact spot — but Cas9 will only cut where the target sits right next to a
> short signal sequence called a PAM that the protein recognizes. Once it binds,
> Cas9 makes a double-strand break in the DNA. The cell's own repair machinery then
> patches the break in one of two ways: a sloppy, error-prone process that usually
> disables the gene, or — if scientists also supply a matching "template" — a
> precise process that can rewrite the DNA to a new, intended sequence.

## Facilitator answer key (don't hand to students)

**Plain-language translation:** CRISPR is a tool, borrowed from how bacteria fight
viruses, that finds one exact spot in DNA and cuts it. A little piece of guide RNA
is custom-made to match the DNA you're targeting and drags the cutting protein
(Cas9) to that address — but only if there's a specific little tag (PAM) right next
to it. Cas9 cuts both strands. Then the cell repairs the cut: either messily, which
breaks the gene (useful if you want to switch a gene off), or — if you hand the
cell a correct copy to follow — neatly, rewriting the gene to what you wanted.

**Terms students will trip on:** RNA-guided (steered by a strand of RNA); guide RNA
(the custom-built "address label"); target sequence (the exact stretch of DNA
you're aiming at); Cas9 (the protein that does the cutting); PAM (a short tag next
to the target that must be present or Cas9 won't cut — a safety check);
double-strand break (a cut through both sides of the DNA ladder); repair machinery
(the cell's built-in fixers); error-prone (makes mistakes); template (a correct
copy to follow when repairing); sequence (the order of DNA letters).

**Prerequisites to build first:** DNA is a long code made of four letters; genes
are stretches of that code; RNA is a related molecule that can match (pair with)
DNA; "match/complementary" is how the guide finds its spot.

**Best analogy + its seam:** Find-and-replace in a document — search for an exact
phrase, then either delete it (gene breaks) or swap in new text (gene rewritten).
*Seam:* find-and-replace always works wherever the phrase appears; CRISPR only cuts
when the PAM tag is next door, and the "replace" step depends on the messy cell
doing the repair, so it isn't guaranteed. Another: GPS (guide RNA) leading scissors
(Cas9) to an address. *Seam:* GPS doesn't refuse to stop unless there's a specific
landmark next door — the PAM requirement has no clean everyday equivalent.

**Common traps:** Thinking Cas9 finds the spot on its own (the guide RNA does the
finding). Forgetting the PAM requirement entirely. Thinking CRISPR *itself*
rewrites the gene — actually the *cell's* repair does, and the precise version only
happens if a template is provided. Mixing up the two repair outcomes.

**A strong present-back sounds like:** "It's molecular scissors. A custom guide RNA
finds one exact DNA spot and brings the Cas9 protein there — but only cuts if a
little PAM tag is right next to it. After the cut, the cell repairs it: messily,
which turns the gene off, or precisely if you give it a template to copy, which
rewrites the gene."
