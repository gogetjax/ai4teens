# Instructor Example: The Fabricated Source (the gasp moment)

Run this live at the start of the session. It is one pre-tested claim that makes
most chatbots do the thing you want the students to see: state a claim with total
confidence, cite it, and get at least one citation wrong or invented.

**Test it yourself the day before on the exact chatbot you will use.** Models
change, and some now hedge or decline to cite. If yours behaves on the day, use the
second claim below, or ask it for "five more sources" until one goes ghost. Have
the search tabs ready so the reveal is thirty seconds, not five minutes.

## The claim
> "Listening to Mozart makes babies smarter."

Almost everyone has heard it. Almost nobody knows where it came from. It is
perfect for the gasp because it has one real source underneath it and a thick
layer of invented ones on top.

## What the AI tends to say (illustrative, your output will differ)
Ask with `prompts/interrogate-the-bot.md`. A typical answer restates the claim
confidently, explains "the Mozart effect," and cites something like:

1. Rauscher, Shaw and Ky (1993), "Music and spatial task performance," *Nature*.
2. A follow-up study on infants, with a named author, a real-sounding journal, and
   a year in the late 1990s or 2000s.
3. A book or review with a plausible title about music and child development.

Read those aloud in your most impressed voice. A real journal. Named authors.
Years. Then open the tabs.

## The reveal
- **Source 1 is real.** Rauscher, Shaw and Ky did publish a short paper in
  *Nature* in 1993. Open it, or the abstract, and read what it actually tested:
  college students, a spatial reasoning task, and an effect that lasted about ten
  to fifteen minutes. Not babies. Not general intelligence. Not permanent.
- **Source 2 is usually a ghost.** Search the exact title in quotes. Search the
  author with the journal. Go to the journal's own archive. Most of the time,
  nothing. Say it plainly: "This paper does not exist. The AI made it up, and it
  made it up in exactly the shape a real one would have."
- **Source 3 varies.** Sometimes real, sometimes a real author attached to a book
  they did not write. Either way, show the search.

Then show the real follow-up the AI usually skips: a 2010 meta-analysis in the
journal *Intelligence* (Pietschnig, Voracek and Formann, "Mozart effect, Shmozart
effect") pooled dozens of studies and found little to no lasting effect. The claim
about babies was never tested by the original paper at all. The infant version
spread through news coverage and a state program that handed out classical music
CDs to new parents.

## The verdicts, on the board
- The claim as stated: **Busted.** The real source does not support it and the
  effect it did find was small, brief, and in adults.
- The AI's citations: at least one **ghost source**, which is a bonus point in the
  rubric when a student catches one.
- The confidence gap: the AI usually sounds like a five. Its accuracy on the claim
  as stated is a one or two. Draw the gap on the board.

## The line to land
"It cited a real paper, and a fake one, in the same tone of voice. You could not
tell them apart by reading. You could only tell by opening them. That is the whole
day."

## Backup claim (if the first one behaves)
> "Students who take handwritten notes remember more than students who type."

There is a real 2014 paper behind it (Mueller and Oppenheimer, *Psychological
Science*). Ask for follow-up studies and replications, and most models will invent
at least one, or attach a real researcher's name to a study that does not exist.
The real 2019 replication attempt (Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson, *Educational
Psychology Review*) found a much weaker and less consistent effect, which makes
this a good Guilty of spin or Mistrial case for a student later.
