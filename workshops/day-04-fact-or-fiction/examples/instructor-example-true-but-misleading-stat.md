# Instructor Example: The True but Misleading Statistic

Use this after `how-a-statistic-lies.md`, or as a second demonstration if the
group needs to see Guilty of spin before they try one. The point of this example
is that nothing in it is fabricated. The number is real, the source is real, the
AI quotes it correctly, and the claim still misleads.

## The claim
> "Teenagers spend nine hours a day on screens."

Students have heard this from parents, teachers, and news segments. Most of them
believe it is exaggerated. Most adults believe it is true. Both are half right.

## What the AI tends to say (illustrative)
Asked to explain and cite, a chatbot usually gives the number confidently and
attributes it to a Common Sense Media census of teen media use. That attribution
is correct. The 2015 Common Sense Census reported that American teens averaged
roughly nine hours a day of entertainment media, and the 2021 update reported a
figure a little under that.

So far, Cleared. Now run `prompts/out-of-how-many.md` on it.

## Where it turns into spin
Walk the five questions with the group and watch the number change shape.

- **What was counted?** Entertainment media only, outside school and homework.
  So the nine hours is on top of any screen use for school, which makes it
  bigger than it sounds. But it also includes listening to music, which most
  people would not call "screen time."
- **Counted how?** The survey counted media multitasking additively in the
  headline figure. An hour of music while gaming could be logged as two hours of
  media. Look for the report's own note on this. The "screen" part of the number
  is smaller than the whole.
- **Out of how many?** It is an average across all teens. The report's own
  breakdown shows a wide spread: some teens far above, a large share well below.
  An average of a lopsided distribution is a number that describes almost nobody.
- **Over what period?** The figure moved between the 2015 and 2021 reports, and
  the 2021 survey was taken during a pandemic year when schooling itself was on
  screens. Which year the claim quotes changes its meaning.
- **Compared to what?** The report also surveyed adults. Ask the students to
  guess the adult figure, then find it. The gap is smaller than the claim
  implies.

## The verdict, on the board
- The number: real, and correctly attributed.
- The claim as people say it: **Guilty of spin.** "Nine hours on screens" takes a
  figure for all entertainment media, including music and double-counted
  multitasking, and presents it as nine hours of staring at a screen.
- The receipt: the report's own definition of what it counted, opened and read
  aloud. That is what earns the points.

## Facilitator notes
- Open the actual report, not a news article about it. The definition section is
  the receipt. A news article that repeats the number without the definition is
  itself an example of the spin.
- Some students will want the verdict to be Busted because the claim annoys them.
  Hold the line. The number is real. Busted is for fabrications and false claims.
  Guilty of spin is for exactly this: true, and misleading.
- If a student asks the AI "is nine hours accurate" and gets a hedged answer with
  the definition caveats, give credit for noticing that the hedged answer was the
  honest one. Confidence is not the same as correctness in either direction.
- Figures and report editions change. Check the latest Common Sense census before
  the session and adjust the numbers above if a newer edition is out.
