# Claim Bank

Two halves. The first half is the student-facing menu: copy it, print it, or put
it on the screen. The second half is the facilitator answer key. Do not hand the
answer key to students working those claims. The point is that they find the
receipts themselves.

Every claim here is real in the sense that people actually say it. Students may
also bring their own claim, which is often better, as long as it is something
they cannot personally settle.

---

## Student menu

### Set A: "Everybody knows"
1. We only use ten percent of our brains.
2. The Great Wall of China is visible from space with the naked eye.
3. Einstein failed math in school.
4. Goldfish have a three-second memory.
5. Cracking your knuckles causes arthritis.
6. Vikings wore horned helmets.
7. Sugar makes kids hyperactive.
8. Lightning never strikes the same place twice.

### Set B: "Studies show"
9. Listening to Mozart makes you smarter.
10. You need to drink eight glasses of water a day.
11. Students who take notes by hand remember more than students who type.
12. Half of all marriages end in divorce.
13. Bananas are radioactive.
14. Humans share about sixty percent of their DNA with bananas.

### Set C: Scary statistics
15. Immigrants commit more crime than people born in the country.
16. Immigrants make up about a third of the population.
17. Crime is higher now than it has ever been.
18. Flying is more dangerous than driving.

Predict your verdict before you start. Then put the bot on trial.

## Facilitator answer key

Verify these before the session. Data updates, links rot, and a claim that was
Busted last year can shift to Guilty of spin when a new dataset lands. The
sources named here are the ones a student should be able to find and open. If a
student finds a better one, that is a point, not a problem.

### Set A: "Everybody knows"

**1. Ten percent of our brains.** Busted. Brain imaging shows activity across
essentially every region over the course of a day, and damage to almost any area
causes some deficit. Point students at neuroscience explainers from a university
or a science magazine, and at Barry Beyerstein's chapter on the myth in *Mind
Myths* (1999). AIs tend to debunk this one correctly, which makes it a good
Cleared-the-debunk case: the student's job is to verify the AI's debunking, not
just accept it.

**2. Great Wall from space.** Busted. Astronaut accounts, including China's own
first astronaut Yang Liwei in 2003, say it cannot be seen with the naked eye from
orbit. NASA has published on this directly. A useful spin variant: it can be
picked out in some satellite and telephoto photos, which is not the same claim.

**3. Einstein failed math.** Busted. His school records show top marks in
mathematics, and he was reportedly shown the claim during his lifetime and denied
it. Walter Isaacson's biography (2007) and the Einstein Archives cover this. Watch
for AIs that "explain" the myth by inventing a specific failed exam.

**4. Goldfish three-second memory.** Busted. Fish learn and retain associations
for weeks to months in controlled studies, and goldfish have been trained to
respond to cues long after training. Search for peer-reviewed work on fish
learning and memory. AIs sometimes cite a specific university experiment with
details that do not check out, which is a ghost-source opportunity.

**5. Knuckle cracking and arthritis.** Busted. Castellanos and Axelrod (1990,
*Annals of the Rheumatic Diseases*) found no association in a survey of older
adults. Donald Unger's sixty-year self-experiment, cracking one hand and not the
other, was published as a letter in *Arthritis and Rheumatism* (1998) and later
won an Ig Nobel Prize. Both are real and findable.

**6. Horned helmets.** Busted. No horned helmet has been found in a Viking-age
context. The image traces to nineteenth-century Romantic art and to Carl Emil
Doepler's costume designs for Wagner's *Ring* cycle in 1876. Museums with Viking
collections have written this up plainly.

**7. Sugar and hyperactivity.** Busted. Wolraich, Wilson and White (1995, *JAMA*)
pooled controlled trials and found no effect of sugar on behavior or cognition in
children. A companion finding students enjoy: Hoover and Milich (1994) told
parents their child had been given sugar when they had not, and the parents rated
the child as more hyperactive anyway.

**8. Lightning never strikes twice.** Busted. The Empire State Building is struck
around twenty times a year, per the National Weather Service. Tall isolated
objects are struck repeatedly by design. Easy receipt, good for a first case.

### Set B: "Studies show"

**9. Mozart makes you smarter.** Busted as stated. See
`instructor-example-fabricated-source.md`. Real source: Rauscher, Shaw and Ky
(1993, *Nature*), college students, a spatial task, a brief effect. Meta-analysis:
Pietschnig, Voracek and Formann (2010, *Intelligence*). Expect ghost sources on
the infant version.

**10. Eight glasses of water.** Guilty of spin at best. Heinz Valtin's review
(2002, *American Journal of Physiology*) found no scientific basis for the
specific "eight by eight" rule. The National Academies (2004) set an adequate
intake for total water, most of which comes from food and all beverages, not
eight separate glasses of plain water. The claim is not crazy, it is just not a
finding.

**11. Handwriting beats typing.** Guilty of spin, or an honest Mistrial. Mueller
and Oppenheimer (2014, *Psychological Science*) is real and found an advantage
for handwriting on conceptual questions. Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson (2019,
*Educational Psychology Review*) attempted a direct replication and found a much
smaller and inconsistent effect. A student who finds both and says "the evidence
is mixed" has earned the Mistrial point and probably a bonus. Expect AIs to invent
supporting replications.

**12. Half of marriages end in divorce.** Guilty of spin. The fifty percent figure
comes from dividing one year's divorces by the same year's marriages, which
compares two different groups of people. Cohort-based estimates are lower, and
divorce rates have fallen since the early 1980s. CDC National Center for Health
Statistics data and Pew Research Center write-ups cover this. This is a clean
"what was counted" case.

**13. Bananas are radioactive.** Cleared, with context. Bananas contain
potassium, a small fraction of which is the radioactive isotope potassium-40.
The dose from one banana is about a tenth of a microsievert, which is trivial
next to background radiation. The "banana equivalent dose" is a real informal
unit used in radiation teaching. A student should clear it and add the context:
true, harmless, and the body regulates potassium anyway.

**14. Sixty percent of DNA shared with bananas.** Mistrial, or Guilty of spin.
The figure is repeated everywhere and traces to loose gene-similarity comparisons
rather than a single peer-reviewed measurement. Depending on whether you compare
whole genomes, protein-coding genes, or proteins, the number swings widely. A
student who searches hard, finds no primary source, and explains why the number
depends on the definition has done the assignment.

### Set C: Scary statistics

Read the facilitator notes in the workshop `README.md` before running these.
State the misconception and the reality in the same breath.

**15. Immigrants commit more crime.** Busted. Abramitzky, Boustan, Jácome, Pérez
and Torres (2023, National Bureau of Economic Research working paper 31440,
"Law-Abiding Immigrants") used census data from 1870 to 2020 and found immigrants
have been incarcerated at lower rates than the US-born throughout, and by a wide
margin today. Light, He and Robey (2020, *Proceedings of the National Academy of
Sciences*) used Texas arrest data and found undocumented immigrants had lower
felony arrest rates than native-born citizens. Both are real, findable, and
written by named researchers at named institutions. The "out of how many"
question is the whole case: raw counts of crimes by immigrants versus the rate
per person.

**16. Immigrants are a third of the population.** Guilty of spin, aimed at the
estimate rather than at any group. Foreign-born residents are roughly one in
seven people in the United States, per the Census Bureau's American Community
Survey. Surveys by YouGov (2022) and Ipsos's "Perils of Perception" series show
people overestimate the size of most minority groups by a lot, and also
overestimate small groups they belong to. Landy, Guay and Marghetis (2018,
*Psychonomic Bulletin and Review*) found the pattern holds across groups and
comes from how people handle proportions: small shares get pushed up, large
shares get pushed down. Lead with that. Everyone does this. The fix is a
denominator, not a lecture.

**17. Crime is higher than ever.** Busted as stated, with a spin variant. FBI
data show the US violent crime rate fell by roughly half from its early-1990s
peak to 2019. Murders rose sharply in 2020 and have fallen since. Gallup has
found that in most years since the 1990s a majority of Americans believed crime
was rising nationally, including years when it fell. Pew Research Center
maintains a plain-language summary, "What the data says about crime in the US,"
that a student can open. The spin variant: a claim that picks 2019 as the start
year and stops in 2021 is accurate and misleading at once. That is the
cherry-picked window from `how-a-statistic-lies.md`.

**18. Flying is more dangerous than driving.** Busted. Per passenger mile, and
per trip, commercial aviation is far safer than driving, per National Safety
Council and Bureau of Transportation Statistics data. The claim survives because
crashes are rare, vivid, and covered heavily. Good for the relative-versus-absolute
trick: a plane crash kills many at once, and the yearly total is still tiny next
to road deaths.

### Using the key
- Circulate with it, ask "show me," and check the receipt against the sources
  here. Do not read the answer to the student. If they are stuck, ask which of the
  three checks or five questions they have not tried yet.
- A student who reaches a different verdict with a better receipt than the one
  listed here wins the point. The key is a floor, not a ceiling.
