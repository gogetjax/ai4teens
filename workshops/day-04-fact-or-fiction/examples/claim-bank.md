# Claim Bank

Two parts. The first is a student-facing menu of claims to put on trial. The second
is a facilitator answer key with the real data and sources for the sensitive
statistics. Do not hand the answer key to a student working those claims.

Pick claims that can be checked in the time you have. Steer clear of live partisan
fights and personal-medical questions, both because they are hard to verify in a
class period and because a claim students feel strongly about turns the game from
"did you verify it" into "do you agree with it."

---

## Part 1 — Student menu (claims to investigate)

### Science and nature myths
- We only use 10 percent of our brains.
- Goldfish have a three-second memory.
- The Great Wall of China is visible from space with the naked eye.
- Bulls are enraged by the color red.
- Cracking your knuckles causes arthritis.
- Bats are blind.
- Lightning never strikes the same place twice.

### Nutrition and health folklore
- Sugar makes kids hyper.
- You must drink eight glasses of water a day.
- Carrots dramatically improve your night vision.
- Swimming right after eating causes cramps that can drown you.
- Swallowed gum stays in your stomach for seven years.

### History and viral claims
- Einstein failed math as a student.
- Vikings wore horned helmets.
- Napoleon was unusually short.
- The people convicted at the Salem witch trials were burned at the stake.
- A famous quote everyone attributes to a historical figure (pick one and check
  whether they actually said it).

### Civics and government (non-partisan, just how it works)
- The President can declare war on his own.
- The phrase "separation of church and state" is written in the Constitution.
- The words "under God" were in the Pledge of Allegiance from the beginning.
- If the police do not read you your rights, your case is automatically thrown out.

### Scary statistics (check the number, find the trick)
- Crime in the country is higher than it was a year ago.
- Immigrants commit more crime than people born here.
- Guess what share of the country belongs to a given group, then check it.

## Part 2 — Facilitator answer key (do not hand out)

Keep the misconception and the reality together when you discuss these. Never leave
a false claim in the air on its own.

### "Crime is higher than a year ago"
Reality: usually the opposite of what people believe. From 2005 to 2024, about 69
percent of Americans on average said crime was higher than the year before, despite
crime falling in most of those years (Council on Criminal Justice, using Gallup
data). In 2023, FBI data showed violent crime down about 8 percent and murder
falling at one of the fastest rates ever recorded (analysis by criminologist Jeff
Asher), while a Gallup poll found 77 percent of Americans thought crime was getting
worse. People also rate national crime as far worse than crime in their own area, a
gap averaging over 40 points for 25 years (Gallup).
Trick: the vivid anecdote (viral clips feel like a trend) and cherry-picked
comparisons (one spiking city or category stands in for the whole).

### "Immigrants commit more crime than people born here"
Reality: research across the political spectrum finds immigrants commit crimes at
lower rates than the U.S.-born. In 2020, immigrants were about 60 percent less
likely to be incarcerated than the U.S.-born (National Bureau of Economic Research).
The pattern holds for the undocumented specifically: about 33 percent less likely to
be incarcerated in one national study, and 47 percent less likely to be convicted in
Texas in 2017. It holds across roughly 150 years of census data. Sources that rarely
agree on immigration agree here: the libertarian Cato Institute found legal
immigrants have the lowest incarceration rates, then illegal immigrants, with
native-born Americans highest; the left-leaning Brennan Center, government
researchers, and academic economists reach the same conclusion.
Trick: raw counts with no denominator, plus anecdote-as-evidence. The teaching bonus
here is cross-source agreement: when Cato and the Brennan Center land in the same
place, that convergence is a lateral-reading lesson by itself.
Facilitator care: frame this as a stereotype the data corrects, not a debate about a
group. There may be immigrant students in the room, and the reality is on their
side. Keep the focus on the missing denominator.

### "What share of the country is [group]"
Reality: people guess wildly high. YouGov found average guesses around 27 percent
Muslim (actually about 1 percent), 30 percent gay or lesbian (about 3 percent), and
41 percent Black (about 12 percent).
Trick and the crucial framing: this is not prejudice. Black Americans overestimate
the Black share of the population too, and people overestimate neutral groups like
homeowners and passport holders. Researchers conclude it comes from general
processes brains use to estimate proportions, not from bias against any group
(PNAS). Lead with that so no student feels indicted. The lesson is that everyone
compresses big numbers and inflates small ones, which is exactly why everyone needs
the habit of checking.

Sources to point students to for verification: Gallup, the FBI's annual crime data,
the National Bureau of Economic Research, the Cato Institute, the Brennan Center,
YouGov, and the PNAS paper on demographic estimates. Part of the lesson is having
students find these themselves rather than taking the numbers on your say-so.
