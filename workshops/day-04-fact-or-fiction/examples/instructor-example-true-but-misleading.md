# Instructor Example — True But Misleading (Guilty of Spin)

> **Facilitator:** The second example, and the subtler one. Here the AI's number is
> real and checks out, but it is missing the context that makes it honest. This
> models the Guilty of spin verdict, the hardest and highest-value catch.

## The shape of the lie
The claim is a real statistic, correctly quoted, that means something very
different once you know what it leaves out. Nothing is fabricated. The trick is
omission. Students who only check "is the number real" will clear it. Students who
ask "out of how many, compared to what, over what span" will catch the spin.

## A worked example: "crime is rising"
Ask the AI whether crime is rising, and it may hand you a real, correctly cited
figure: a specific city, a specific category, a specific year where a number went
up. All true. All checkable. And all misleading if the honest picture is a long
decline.
- The receipt for the spin: from 2005 to 2024, about 69 percent of Americans on
  average said crime was higher than the year before, despite crime falling in most
  of those years (Council on Criminal Justice, using Gallup). In 2023, violent
  crime fell about 8 percent and murder dropped at one of the fastest rates on
  record (FBI data, as analyzed by criminologist Jeff Asher), even as 77 percent of
  Americans thought crime was getting worse (Gallup).
- The missing context: a single spiking number in one place or category is real but
  does not describe the trend. Cherry-picked comparison, plus the vivid-anecdote
  effect.

## A second worked example: "how big is that group"
Ask an AI, or the room, what share of the U.S. is some minority group. The gut
answer is wildly high. YouGov found average guesses around 27 percent Muslim
(actually about 1 percent), 30 percent gay or lesbian (about 3 percent), 41 percent
Black (about 12 percent). The honest framing, which the scary version drops: this
overestimation is nearly universal and not about prejudice. People overestimate
their own group and neutral groups alike; researchers trace it to how brains handle
proportions (PNAS). The misleading version uses the inflated guess to imply
something is bigger, and therefore more threatening, than it is.

## The verdict this models
Guilty of spin. The student shows the number is true, then produces the missing
denominator, trend, or context that flips its meaning. Worth top points because it
is the catch most people miss.
