# How a Statistic Lies (mini-lesson)

Teach this before the Scary Statistics claims. A number can be completely true and
still lie, and these are the five moves that do it. Each one comes with a real,
documented example so students see the trick in the wild. Every example is a case
where checking the number defuses a scary story, not builds one.

## 1. Out of how many? (the missing denominator)
A raw count with no base rate is the most common way a true number misleads. "There
were X arrests" means nothing until you ask X out of how many people, compared to
whom. The fix is one question: out of how many?
Example: many people believe immigrants commit more crime than the U.S.-born. When
you put it as a rate, the opposite shows up. In 2020, immigrants were about 60
percent less likely to be incarcerated than the U.S.-born (National Bureau of
Economic Research), and studies of undocumented immigrants specifically find lower
rates too (about 33 percent less likely to be incarcerated in one national study;
47 percent less likely to be convicted in Texas in 2017). The scary version drops
the denominator.

## 2. Count versus rate
A bigger group will rack up more total anything, just by being bigger. Total counts
punish large groups and reward small ones. Always compare rates (per 100,000), not
totals, when groups are different sizes.

## 3. Cherry-picked comparison
Pick the one year, one city, or one subgroup where the number spikes, and quietly
ignore the rest. A claim that leans on a single point in time or place is a red
flag. Ask: compared to what, and over what span?
Example: people have believed crime is rising for decades even while it fell. From
2005 to 2024, about 69 percent of people on average said crime was higher than the
year before, despite crime falling in most of those years (Council on Criminal
Justice, using Gallup). In 2023, 77 percent thought crime was worsening while FBI
data showed violent crime down about 8 percent.

## 4. Correlation is not cause
Two things moving together does not mean one caused the other. Ice cream sales and
drowning both rise in summer; ice cream does not cause drowning, heat does. When a
claim jumps from "linked" to "caused," slow down.

## 5. The vivid anecdote (availability)
One shocking video or story feels like a trend, because your brain remembers what is
vivid, not what is common. This is why crime feels like it is everywhere even where
it is falling. Tellingly, people rate crime in the whole country as far worse than
crime in their own neighborhood, a gap that has averaged over 40 points for 25
years (Gallup). They feel safe where they actually live, and scared about the places
they only see on a screen.

## Bonus: your brain shrinks big numbers and grows small ones
Ask the room to guess what share of Americans are Muslim, or gay, or Black. People
guess wildly high: on average around 27 percent Muslim (actually about 1 percent),
30 percent gay or lesbian (about 3 percent), 41 percent Black (about 12 percent),
per YouGov. Here is the key: this is not prejudice. Black Americans overestimate the
Black share too, and people overestimate totally neutral groups like homeowners.
Researchers find it comes from how brains handle proportions, not from bias against
anyone (PNAS). Everyone in the room does it, which is exactly why everyone needs the
habit of checking.
